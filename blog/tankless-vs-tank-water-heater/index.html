    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in Kansas

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 5 min read

    How a Tank Water Heater Works

    A standard tank heater keeps 40 or 50 gallons hot around the clock. The design is simple, replacement is cheap, and a swap usually takes a few hours. The downside is standby loss. The tank loses heat through its walls all day, which means the heater fires even when no one is running water.

    Water across much of Kansas runs quite hard, leaving mineral scale in water heaters, faucets, showerheads, and dishwashers.

    Tanks typically last 8 to 12 years. Inside the tank, an anode rod corrodes so the steel does not, and when the rod is used up the tank begins to rust. Replacing the anode every few years can add years of life. Few homeowners ever do it.

    How Tankless Units Work

    A tankless heater fires only on demand. Water passes through a heat exchanger and comes out hot in a few seconds, with no stored water to keep warm. You never run out, which is the main reason people switch. The limit is flow, since a unit can only raise the temperature on so many gallons per minute.

    Incoming water temperature sets how much hot water a tankless unit can deliver. In winter, colder incoming water can drop a 9 gallon per minute unit to 5 or 6. That is enough for one shower and a dishwasher, but maybe not two showers and a washer. Size for winter.

    Cost and Installation

    Swapping a tank is mostly a like-for-like job. Tankless installs usually cost more because the gas line and venting both change, and sometimes the electrical does too. Most whole-house gas tankless units need 150,000 to 199,000 BTUs at full fire, which an existing half-inch gas line often cannot supply. Upsizing to 3/4 inch is common.

    Venting changes too. Condensing tankless units vent through PVC, while non-condensing units need stainless steel vent pipe. Our installers at KRV Plumbing check the gas line size and the vent route before we quote anything, so there are no surprises on install day. Condensing units also need a drain for the acidic condensate, usually through a neutralizer.

    Water heater replacements, repipes, sewer line work, and new fixtures usually require a local permit and an inspection.

    Either type needs a temperature and pressure relief valve piped to a safe spot. On a closed system with a check valve or pressure reducing valve, an expansion tank is usually required with a tank heater. Permits are standard for both kinds of installs. We pull them.

    Which One Fits Your Kansas Home

    A tank still makes sense for plenty of houses. In a two-bath house where budget matters most, a same-size replacement tank is usually the easy call. Tankless pays off best in homes where people run out of hot water now, or where the old tank sits in a tight closet you want back. Space is a real factor.

    Tankless units often run 20 years with yearly maintenance, about twice what a tank lasts. With hard water, plan on a yearly flush of the heat exchanger using a small pump and descaling solution. Skip it and the unit will throw error codes within a few years. Gas units also need a clean air intake.

    Getting a Straight Answer

    Bring us the age of your current heater, the number of people in the house and whether anyone has ever run out of hot water mid-shower. We check the gas line and vent path and then quote both options next to each other. KRV Plumbing installs both types across Kansas, and you can reach the office at (316) 229-8459. Most tank swaps are done the same day.

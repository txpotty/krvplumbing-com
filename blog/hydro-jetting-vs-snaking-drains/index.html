    Hydro Jetting vs. Snaking: Which Drain Cleaning Method Do You Need?

    By Aaron Mitchell · August 22, 2026 · 6 min read

    What Is Actually in the Pipe

    Before we pick a tool, we want to know what the clog is made of. A sudden stop after wipes go down is not the same job as a kitchen drain that has been getting slower for months. Roots and grease respond to different methods. The history tells us a lot.

    Slab leaks and cracked sewer lines are common results of shifting clay, especially after long dry spells followed by heavy rain.

    Ground movement opens joints, and open joints let roots in. A root mass in a sewer line is one of the most common reasons a cable machine comes back out wrapped in fibers. That tells us the job is bigger than a clear-out. Roots regrow fast.

    Drain Snaking: The Standard First Step

    A drain snake, also called an auger or a cable machine, is a long coiled steel cable with a cutting head on the end. We feed it into the drain, the motor spins it, and the head bores through the clog. Most calls take under an hour. For a sink or tub, a small handheld unit with quarter-inch or 3/8 cable does the job. Main lines get a larger machine with 1/2 or 3/4 inch cable and blades sized to the pipe.

    • Good for: hair, wipes, a single hard blockage, light roots
    • Limits: opens a channel but leaves grease and scale on the pipe walls
    • Price: usually the lower-cost option

    The weakness is that a snake opens a hole but does not clean the pipe. A 4-inch line coated in grease might get a 2-inch channel through the middle, which flows fine for a few weeks. After that, the leftover grease grabs more debris and it clogs again. Repeat calls are the sign.

    Hydro Jetting: Cleaning the Whole Pipe

    A hydro jetter pushes water through a hose at 3,000 to 4,000 psi on a typical residential line. The nozzle has rear-facing jets that pull it forward up the line and scour the pipe wall all the way around as it goes. It cuts grease and sludge off the walls. Roots get sliced off flush with the joint.

    Many Kansas homeowners use water softeners to extend the life of their tank water heaters and fixtures against heavy mineral buildup.

    Scale and grease are where jetting really earns its cost. Mineral buildup inside cast iron narrows the pipe and gives grease something to grip. A descaling nozzle on the jetter brings the pipe back near its original size, so it stays clear for a year or longer, not a few weeks. Commercial kitchens jet on a schedule.

    • Good for: grease, sludge, scale, recurring roots
    • Limits: needs a camera check first on old or fragile pipe

    Choosing Between Them

    A first-time clog in a single fixture almost always gets a snake. It is faster and costs less, and usually that is all it takes. A line that clogs again within a few months, or one that a cable cannot get through, gets a camera and then usually a jetter. The camera decides.

    Homes from the late seventies through the mid nineties may have polybutylene supply pipe, which is prone to sudden failure.

    Pipe condition limits the choice. Jetting at full pressure inside a cracked clay line or thin, rusted cast iron can make a bad pipe worse. On older lines we camera first, then jet at lower pressure or snake if the pipe is too far gone. PVC and newer cast iron handle full pressure fine.

    Booking the Right Service

    Tell the dispatcher which drains are slow and how long it has been happening. That one detail usually tells us which truck to send.

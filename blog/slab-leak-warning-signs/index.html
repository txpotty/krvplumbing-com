    Slab Leak Warning Signs Every Kansas Homeowner Should Know

    By Aaron Mitchell · October 15, 2026 · 5 min read

    Pour a slab, lay soft copper or PEX in the gravel underneath, and you get a house that is cheap to build and solid for decades. A large share of homes from the last fifty years were built exactly that way. The catch is simple. When one of those lines opens up, nothing shows on the surface, and the water soaks into the base gravel for weeks before anybody notices. Catching it early keeps a pinhole from becoming a flooring and mold job.

    Why Slab Leaks Happen in Kansas Homes

    Under-slab copper fails for mechanical reasons first. Builders from the 1970s into the early 2000s often laid soft copper straight in the trench before the pour. Over thirty or forty years, that tubing rubs against gravel and rebar every time it moves, and the wall wears thin at the contact point. Soil adds its own stress.

    Slab leaks and broken sewer laterals are common results of shifting clay, especially after long dry spells followed by heavy rain.

    Expansive clay swells when it gets wet and shrinks back in a dry spell. Every swell and shrink cycle shoves the pipe against whatever rock sits next to it. Buried stones and hard pan act as pressure points, and a copper line pinned against one of them takes a little more abuse each season until the wall gives out at that spot. It all happens out of sight.

    Water chemistry plays a part too. Hard water and the chlorine or chloramine that utilities add for disinfection can pit copper from the inside, leaving tiny craters that eventually go all the way through. Hot lines usually fail first. They expand and contract every time a faucet opens, which grinds the pipe against the concrete.

    Private wells serve many rural homes, and minerals, sediment, and nitrate levels make periodic well testing a good habit.

    The Warning Signs to Watch For

    Slab leaks almost never show up as puddles. What you get instead is a handful of small clues that add up. Here is what we ask about on the first phone call:

    • A warm spot on the floor. A leaking hot line heats the concrete above it, and bare feet on tile tend to notice it before anything else.
    • The meter moving with everything off. Shut off every fixture, the irrigation and the ice maker, then watch the small triangle or star dial on the meter for 15 minutes.
    • A bill that jumps for no reason. Compare it with the same month last year, since irrigation season skews month to month comparisons.
    • Running water you can hear under the floor or behind a wall when nothing is on.
    • A water heater that never seems to rest, because it keeps refilling with cold water to replace what is leaking out.
    • Damp carpet pad or cupped hardwood with no leak you can point to.
    • A musty smell along the baseboards, especially in interior rooms with no plumbing nearby.
    • New cracks in tile or drywall that show up alongside any of the signs above.

    One clue by itself proves nothing. Put two together, say a meter that creeps overnight and a warm patch in the hallway, and it is time to schedule leak detection before the water reaches the drywall. We see the meter test catch leaks that people had been living with for months.

    Finding the Leak Without Tearing Up the House

    Leak detection is a lot less destructive than most people picture. The first hour goes to splitting the system into hot and cold and putting a gauge on each side to find the line that will not hold. Then the listening equipment comes out, a ground microphone that picks up the hiss of water forcing its way out of a pinhole. Thermal cameras help on the hot side. Most of the time we can mark the spot within a foot or two before any concrete gets cut.

    Then you pick a fix. A spot repair means cutting a small opening in the slab, replacing the bad section and patching the concrete. A reroute abandons the leaking line under the slab and runs new PEX through the attic or walls instead. When the house has had two or three slab leaks already, or the original copper is clearly worn out, a whole-house repipe is the honest answer. Spot repairs often run about $1,500 to $4,000 depending on access and flooring, and a single-line reroute tends to fall somewhere from $1,000 to $3,500.

    What to Do in the Meantime

    Find your main shutoff today, not at midnight with water coming up through the grout. In a lot of houses it sits near the front hose bib or in a curb box out by the street, under a lid you may need a screwdriver to pop. Should the meter test confirm the leak and you see water coming up, shut the main off right away. If the hot side is the problem, put the water heater on vacation or pilot so the burner or elements do not run with the supply shut off. Take photos of any damaged flooring. Many homeowners policies pay to access and repair the leak even when they exclude the pipe itself.

    When to Call a Professional

    This is not a weekend project. Cutting concrete without knowing exactly where the leak is wastes a day, and many newer slabs have post-tension cables that you do not want to hit. Our techs pin down the leak first, then price the spot repair and the reroute side by side before any concrete is cut.

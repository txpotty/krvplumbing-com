    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 6 min read

    The pipes you never see do some of the hardest work in the house. The water service line runs from the meter to the house, usually 3/4 or 1 inch pipe buried below the frost line. A sewer lateral carries everything from the drains out to the city main or the septic tank. Both sit several feet underground, and either one can fail with very little warning. Fixing one rarely means trenching the yard anymore.

    What Damages Buried Pipes in Kansas

    Tree roots cause more sewer trouble than anything else. Willows and silver maples are the worst offenders, though plenty of ornamental trees go after sewer lines too. A hairline crack or loose joint leaks warm, nutrient-rich vapor, and roots find that vapor and work their way inside. Inside, they grow into a mat that snags waste and can eventually split the pipe.

    Much of Kansas has expansive clay soil that swells when wet and shrinks in drought, which can move foundations and stress buried pipes.

    Soil movement does damage slowly. Clay that swells after rain and shrinks in a drought keeps working against buried rock and hard layers that do not move at all. Over years that cycle shifts joints and cracks rigid pipe, leaving sags, which plumbers call bellies, where waste sits and builds up. Heavy trucks parked over a shallow line and poor bedding during construction make it worse.

    Pipe material matters as well. In older neighborhoods it is common to find the original clay tile or cast iron sewer still in the ground. Some houses also have galvanized steel water service lines that rust from the inside out. Homes built from the 1970s on usually have ABS or PVC sewer pipe, which does not corrode but can still crack, pull apart at the joints or flatten under load.

    A lot of Kansas housing dates from the mid-century building boom, with galvanized supply lines and cast iron drains.

    Warning Signs of Underground Pipe Damage

    Buried pipe problems show up in roundabout ways. Watch for these:

    • Repeat backups in more than one fixture, starting with whatever drain sits lowest.
    • A gurgling toilet when the washing machine drains.
    • Sewer smell in the yard or around a cleanout.
    • A green or soggy strip of lawn that stands out in a dry spell.
    • Sinkholes or depressions along the path of the line.
    • A water meter that keeps moving with every fixture off, pointing to a service line leak.
    • Low water pressure through the whole house.

    Modern Repair Methods

    Diagnosis comes first. For a sewer line, we push a waterproof camera through the cleanout and record the full run. The footage shows roots, cracks, offsets and bellies, and a locator at the camera head marks the depth and location of each one from the surface. On a water service line, we pressure test it and listen with acoustic gear to find the leak.

    Cured-in-place lining slides a resin-soaked felt liner into the old sewer, inflates it and lets it cure into a new jointless pipe inside the old one. On a 4 inch lateral, the cured liner takes up very little of the inside diameter, and it flows better than rough cast iron did. Cracks and root entry points get sealed over, and most jobs need nothing more than the cleanout for access. Pipe bursting pulls new HDPE through the line while a cone-shaped head breaks the old pipe outward. Bursting suits pipe too broken to line. Spot repair means digging up one section to replace a single broken joint or belly. For water service, directional boring runs a new line from meter to house with a small pit at each end.

    Cost comes down to footage and depth. Expect $150 to $400 for the camera inspection itself. Spot sewer repairs often land between $1,500 and $4,500, trenchless lining or bursting of a full residential lateral commonly runs about $4,000 to $12,000, and a new water service line is roughly $1,500 to $5,000. Trenchless is not always cheaper per foot than an excavator, but you are not paying to rebuild the driveway and the pavers afterward.

    When to Call a Professional

    Drains that keep backing up, or a meter showing a leak you cannot find, call for a professional inspection. Underground work often needs permits and utility locates, and careful digging around gas lines.

    How to Protect Your Pipes Before the Next Kansas Freeze

    By Andrew Blake · October 6, 2026 · 5 min read

    How Much Freeze Risk You Actually Have

    Water expands about nine percent when it freezes. That expansion rarely splits the pipe at the ice plug itself. Pressure builds between the ice and a closed faucet, and the pipe gives way in that trapped stretch, which is why burst lines often show up a few feet from the coldest spot and only start spraying once things thaw. A single cold night can do it.

    Hard freezes happen every winter in Kansas, and pipes in exterior walls, garages, and crawlspaces are the most at risk.

    Pipe in an unheated crawlspace, an exterior wall, an attic or a garage is at the most risk, and so is anything on a wall that takes the wind head on. Those areas can sit well below freezing while the living room reads 68. That is where the work begins.

    Outdoor Plumbing Comes First

    Disconnect every garden hose before the first freeze. A hose left on a spigot traps water in the valve body and splits it. Frost-free sillcocks have a long stem that shuts the water off inside the heated wall, but they only work if the hose comes off so the barrel can drain. For an old-style spigot, close the inside stop valve and open the outdoor tap to drain it.

    Sprinkler systems need their own shutdown. Shut the main irrigation valve, then crack the test cocks on the backflow device so it drains. Lines buried below the frost line usually come through fine, but the backflow assembly sits above ground, and a cracked brass body on a pressure vacuum breaker is one of the most common spring repair calls we get. In long-winter areas, blowing the lines out with compressed air is cheap insurance.

    Protecting Pipes Inside the House

    Foam pipe sleeves are cheap and work. Match the sleeve to the pipe, which is usually 1/2 or 3/4 inch in a house. Slide them over every exposed run in the crawlspace and garage, tape the seams, and pay extra attention to elbows and tees, since a gap at a fitting leaves the most vulnerable part of the line bare. On runs that froze before, a listed self-regulating heat cable under the foam is worth the money.

    On the coldest nights, open the kitchen and bathroom cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls so house heat reaches the pipes. Let a faucet on an exterior wall drip, hot and cold both. Moving water is slow to freeze, and an open tap relieves the pressure that actually bursts the pipe. If you travel, set the heat no lower than 55 degrees. Keep the garage door shut.

    Crawlspace vents and gaps around pipe penetrations let cold air blow straight onto the plumbing. Shut or cover the foundation vents once nights turn cold. Seal holes where pipes pass through rim joists or exterior walls with expanding foam or caulk, because a pencil-sized gap with wind behind it can freeze a pipe inside an otherwise warm wall. Most people never look there.

    Find your main shutoff before winter. Most homes have one where the supply line enters the house and another at the meter. Turn it once a year so it does not seize, and if it is an old gate valve that will not close all the way, have it swapped for a quarter-turn ball valve, which takes a plumber about an hour on most homes. A stuck shutoff turns a small burst into a soaked floor.

    Water heaters in a garage or crawlspace need attention too. Wrap the first few feet of the hot and cold lines coming off the tank. A heater that sits in an unheated space and loses power or gas during a cold snap can freeze in its own lines, so know how to shut off the cold supply and open a hot tap to relieve pressure if that happens. Do not drain it unless you have to.

    When to Call a Professional

    No water from a faucet on a freezing morning means a line is frozen somewhere upstream. Leave the faucet open so melting water has somewhere to go. Warm the suspect section slowly with a hair dryer or a space heater kept well back, and never use an open flame, because a torch on a frozen copper line can start a fire inside the wall or split the pipe from steam. Shut the main right away if the frozen spot is buried in a wall or a ceiling starts to drip, then reach KRV Plumbing at (316) 229-8459. A thawed line still needs a pressure check for splits before the water goes back on.

    Fall Inspections Worth Scheduling

    A fall walkthrough takes about an hour. The tech checks exposed runs, hose bibs, the water heater and the main shutoff. Spots that froze in past winters get a closer look, and that is often the moment to talk about rerouting a line out of an exterior wall or replacing an old spigot with a frost-free model long enough to reach heated space. Fixing it before the cold costs a fraction of an emergency call.
